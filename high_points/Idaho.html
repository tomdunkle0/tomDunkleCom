Borah Peak (Idaho)

Elevation: 12,662ft / 3,859m
Date Completed: August 3, 2025
Chronology: High point #43 of 50

Figure 1: Borah Peak's summit, as seen from the Borah Peak Trail, just before Chicken-out Ridge.

Figure 2: Tom and Ry posing at the summit of Borah Peak.

Idaho's Borah Peak is a gem of an accomplishment for any high-pointer. It is incredibly steep: the trail is only about 3.8 miles long, but there is approximately a mile of vertical elevation gain. The trail only has two sections that feel somewhat flat, otherwise the whole thing is steep. Reaching the summit also requires climbing across the infamous "Chicken-out Ridge". The ridge is either a class 3 or class 4 scramble (on the Yosemite Decimal System), as sources I've looked at are divided. I'd say it's mostly 3, but there are parts that feel 4-ish.

Figure 3: Borah Peak, as seen from Birch Creek Road, while approaching the Borah Peak Campground.

Figure 4: The plaque at the Borah Peak trailhead.

Borah's trailhead gets crowded during the day. The parking lot at the trailhead is bordered by about 5 or 6 primitive campsites, and there are a few more along the road leading to the parking lot. Interestingly, despite the crowd during the day, there were not many people camping besides Ry and I, and we had no problem grabbing a campsite when we showed up at 4:30pm on a Saturday. I wouldn't bet on always being that lucky, though.

I was asleep by 7:00pm. I was awoken around 9:00pm by a loud, yelling guy near my tent. I could hear that he was one of a group of people. From what I gathered, "Loud Guy" (LG) was hiking the peak with his Dad and another person--those three had just made it back. They also had a fourth guy who hadn't made it back to the trailhead yet. For some reason, the group returned to the campsite with the knowledge that LG wouldn't be able to get into his car. I am guessing that he lost his key fob on the mountain?

LG kept yelling, swearing, and talking about how bad the situation was. The lock for the physical key on the driver's side door was broken, and so was the one on the trunk. The fob was the only way he could unlock the car. After much discussion and pondering, LG and his Dad finally agreed that they would need to intentionally break a window on LG's car to get in. His Dad did the honors.

That wasn't all! Throughout all of this, LG also kept yelling, "JAAAAAREEEEED!!!". Jared was the fourth guy, who hadn't made it back yet. After smashing the window, I heard LG explain to somebody else at the campsite that their fourth member still hadn't made it back. LG pointed out which car he drives, explained what he looked like, and asked the friendly stranger to tell Jared that his group had left already when he got back. LG also mentioned that they were worried because Jared is 'elderly'. Before the group got in LG's car to leave, LG yelled one last time, "PEACE OUT JARED!!". They drove off, the campsite fell dead silent, and I drifted off to sleep.

We woke up at 2:30am, and were the first awake at the campsite. We quietly got ready, and hit the trail at 3:30am. The starting temperature was warm: low 60's (F) even though it was the middle of the night. From the very start, the trail is steep and quickly gets steeper. The first mile and a half of the trail is mostly this steep incline through the forest, until you get to the tree line.

Figure 7: The summit of Borah Peak, and Chicken-out Ridge (on the right side of the photo).

Figure 8: The base of neighboring Mt. Idaho, showing very steep slopes with some tree growth.

Once above the tree line, there is an theatric section of the trail that's actually mellow and somewhat flat. Along this stretch, the mountain's summit is visible, alongside Chicken-out Ridge and an extremely steep rock shelf rising up to it. The rock shelf is scarier from a distance than up close, but it shows how dramatically the mountain rises up to Chicken-out Ridge.

Figure 9: Mount Idaho, just a couple miles south of Borah Peak.

Figure 10: A closer shot of Mt. Idaho.

Along the way, another theatric element to this section is the view of neighboring Mt. Idaho. Mt. Idaho is more difficult to climb than Borah Peak. Its northwest face is stunning, both in its steepness and in the multi-colored vertical stripes along it. At some point, I would love to come back to climb it.

After taking our first food break at the saddle, we continued hiking up via the Queens Canyon Trail. Although it's the less steep route to the summit, the Queens Canyon Trail is still pretty steep where it ascends from the saddle to a major foothill of Boundary Peak. In less than an hour and with not too much effort, we'd climbed high enough to take the photos below. The trail is visible if you zoom in on either of the photos.

Figure 15: The valley just west of Borah Peak, as soon from above.

Figure 16: The saddle of Borah Peak, as seen from above after just crossing over it.

After hiking up for about an hour, we reached the top of the foothill and got our first real, up-close view of the summit of Boundary Peak (i.e. Figure 1 at the beginning of this page). It was an amazing thing to see in person. At this point, leg two of the hike was complete. We hung out on some rocks eating jerky and dried fruit, contemplating how steep the trail ahead looked. But, we couldn't get a clear read on how spicy it would be, looking at it from this angle.

Figure 17: Another view of the saddle of Borah Peak, as seen from above after just crossing over it.

Figure 18: Ry scrambling across rocks on the final pitch of the mountain.

It wasn't as bad as we'd feared, although there was quite a lot of boulder scrambling to get to the top. There are never any real cliffs on this hike. Nowhere you could get killed with one false move, but it's still very hard work climbing up boulders this size at such a steep angle.

Right next to the summit of Boundary Peak are two large rock formations that look like druids engaging in a ceremony. They came closer and closer into our view as we approached the summit. From this angle, the one on the right looks like a druid kneeling down, while the one on the left looks like he's accepting some sort of offering from the one on the right. Or... maybe it even looks like a marriage proposal. Very interesting visual.

At the summit are two different geographic markers. And, confusingly, neither of them seems to be on the highest rock on the summit. But either way, it's definitely the highest point in Nevada. As with many of the state high points, there's a box full of notebooks and comments from previous climbers to look through.
