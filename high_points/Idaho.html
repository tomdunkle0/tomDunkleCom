Borah Peak (Idaho)

Elevation: 12,662ft / 3,859m
Date Completed: August 3, 2025
Chronology: High point #43 of 50

Figure 1: Borah Peak's summit, as seen from the Borah Peak Trail, just before Chicken-out Ridge.

Figure 2: Tom and Ry posing at the summit of Borah Peak.

Idaho's Borah Peak is a gem of an accomplishment for any high-pointer. It is incredibly steep: the trail is only about 3.8 miles long, but there is approximately a mile of vertical elevation gain. The trail only has two sections that feel somewhat flat, otherwise the whole thing is steep. Reaching the summit also requires climbing across the infamous "Chicken-out Ridge". The ridge is either a class 3 or class 4 scramble (on the Yosemite Decimal System), as sources I've looked at are divided. I'd say it's mostly 3, but there are parts that feel 4-ish.

Figure 3: Borah Peak, as seen from Birch Creek Road, while approaching the Borah Peak Campground.

Figure 4: The plaque at the Borah Peak trailhead.

Borah's trailhead gets crowded during the day. The parking lot at the trailhead is bordered by about 5 or 6 primitive campsites, and there are a few more along the road leading to the parking lot. Interestingly, despite the crowd during the day, there were not many people camping besides Ry and I, and we had no problem grabbing a campsite when we showed up at 4:30pm on a Saturday. I wouldn't bet on always being that lucky, though.

I was asleep by 7:00pm. I was awoken around 9:00pm by a loud, yelling guy near my tent. I could hear that he was one of a group of people. From what I gathered, "Loud Guy" (LG) was hiking the peak with his Dad and another person--those three had just made it back. They also had a fourth guy who hadn't made it back to the trailhead yet. For some reason, the group returned to the campsite with the knowledge that LG wouldn't be able to get into his car. I am guessing that he lost his key fob on the mountain?

LG kept yelling, swearing, and talking about how bad the situation was. The lock for the physical key on the driver's side door was broken, and so was the one on the trunk. The fob was the only way he could unlock the car. After much discussion and pondering, LG and his Dad finally agreed that they would need to intentionally break a window on LG's car to get in. His Dad did the honors.

That wasn't all! Throughout all of this, LG also kept yelling, "JAAAAAREEEEED!!!". Jared was the fourth guy, who hadn't made it back yet. After smashing the window, I heard LG explain to somebody else at the campsite that their fourth member still hadn't made it back. LG pointed out which car he drives, explained what he looked like, and asked the friendly stranger to tell Jared that his group had left already when he got back. LG also mentioned that they were worried because Jared is 'elderly'. Before the group got in LG's car to leave, LG yelled one last time, "PEACE OUT JARED!!". They drove off, the campsite fell dead silent, and I drifted off to sleep.

For the rest of the trip, I kept joking about finding a dead old guy along the trail, but luckily we never did.

We woke up at 2:30am, and were the first awake at the campsite. We quietly got ready, and hit the trail at 3:30am. The starting temperature was warm: low 60's (F) even though it was the middle of the night. From the very start, the trail is steep and quickly gets steeper. The first mile and a half of the trail is mostly this steep incline through the forest, until you get to the tree line.

Figure 7: The summit of Borah Peak, and Chicken-out Ridge (on the right side of the photo).

Figure 8: The base of neighboring Mt. Idaho, showing very steep slopes with some tree growth.

Once above the tree line, there is a theatric section of the trail that's mellow and somewhat flat. Along this stretch, the mountain's summit is visible, alongside Chicken-out Ridge and an extremely steep rock shelf rising up to it. The rock shelf is scarier from a distance than up close, but it shows how dramatically the mountain rises up to Chicken-out Ridge.

Figure 9: Mount Idaho, just a couple miles south of Borah Peak.

Figure 10: A closer shot of Mt. Idaho.

Along the way, another theatric element to this section is the view of neighboring Mt. Idaho. Mt. Idaho is more difficult to climb than Borah Peak. Its northwest face is stunning, both in its steepness and in the multi-colored vertical stripes along it. At some point, I would love to come back to climb it.

Figure 11: A closer shot of the summit of Borah Peak, from the southwest ridge.

Figure 12: A closer shot of Chicken-out Ridge.

After appreciating the views of the summit and the ridge for long enough, we began the steep climb up to the ridge. It's not as bad as it looks, but once we crawled through a narrow notch at the top of the incline, we were suddenly faced with the ridge: the real part that makes people chicken out.

Traversing the ridge quickly became my favorite part of the whole hike. I'd been working my way up to the ridges like this in recent years. I'd describe being on the ridge as moderately scary. But, the fun factor is unreal in comparison. I got virtually no photographs on the ridge--rather, I used my GoPro camera to film the entire traverse in each direction. So, while I'm going to initially post this page with no direct footage of the ridge, stay tuned for lots of ridge footage in an upcoming episiode of my YouTube show, Walking Up Hills. When finished, I'll embed the episode here on this page, too.

The ridge ends with a downclimb of about 25 feet using a rope that somebody reinstalls every season. The rope isn't strictly necessary, but it helps (moreso on the way up than down).

Figure 13: Ry taking pictures during the final pitch, just above the saddle.

Figure 14: The southwest ridge (which the Borah Peak trail traverses) from above, looking back.

After descending the rope, I had mixed feelings. I felt great about completing the mountain's crux, but my mistake of not eating enough throughout the day of the climb caught up to me at this point. I sat down and dry heaved for several minutes, and finally vomited: first once, then a second time. After several minutes resting, and slowly forcing some snacks, I decided to rally and keep going. While it was a successful climb, I was slow and exceptionally careful from the vomit sesh onward.

It's interesting how the body can send signals like that. My body got so tired of malnourishment that it used vomiting to signal to me that my own stomach was empty. Hilarious, and effective.

Figure 15: The final pitch to the summit of Borah Peak.

Figure 16: The final pitch to the summit of Borah Peak, zoomed in.

After crossing Chicken-out Ridge, the trail is gentle and flat again for just about a quarter- to half-mile. Then, the final pitch up to the summit is a steep hike of loose rocks of all sizes. It's a pain in the ass coming up and going down. Trekking poles helped on the way down, and I would also recommend a helmet for both this section and Chicken-out Ridge.

Despite how steep the mountain is, the summit is wide and perfect for comfortably resting. We ate some chicken sandwiches, talked with a few other people at the summit, and traded a guy grandma candies in exchange for cheese. The weather was absolutely perfect: sunny, clear and almost no wind. The forecast said it was about 35°F, but it felt warmer due to the clear conditions. After about a half hour, we started back down.

The descent of Chicken-out Ridge was just as fun as the ascent, although admittedly I was still tired and feeling low on nourishment. We talked to several people along the ridge. One guy was a local who told us that he'd climbed Borah nine times before. He told us that we looked comfortable on the ridge and looked like we knew what we were doing. I'll take that as a compliment.

If you absolutely want to climb this mountain without crossing Chicken-out Ridge, there technically is a section of the trail that bypasses the ridge. To be honest, though, I really think that trail is harder (and probably more dangerous) than crossing the ridge. The last section of it is a very steep hike up loose talus, and you could slide down easily.
